Project 2 Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Ruofei Mao — rumao@ucsd.edu

Proposition: Abortion demand in the United States is significantly dominated by just a few states, with a small number of them accounting for the vast majority of procedures.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Descending Color Strengths: Score = 1.5
    I used flare_r (dark-to-light) color palatte to emphasize the difference in number of abortions. Darker hues help draw immediate attention to high-number states (CA, NY, TX, etc.), visually highlighting the "dominance" narrative.
  • Horizontal Bar Chart Sorting: Score = 1
    I chose to sort the bars in descending order to creates a "long tail" effect, exaggerating the gap between top states and others (especially lower states). Compared to an unsorted chart, this method increased visual emphasis on the top 5 states in abortion.
  • National Average Line: Score = 2
    I encorportated a dotted red line to show the national average abortion number and sort of "cut the states in half" on whether they exceed the average or not. 12 states appear as exceeding average, framing the majority as "below average." and making viewers think abortion rate is only high in a few outlier states.
  • Use of Data: Score = -1
    I directly used the total number of abortions done in each states to emphasize the proposition. However, this is a deceptive use as this graph did not account for difference in population. For example, California could have had a leading number of abortion because it has a very high population to begin with, compared to South Dakota.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Choice of Binning Steps Score = -0.5
    I choose to have a bin step going up by 4 each time (ex. 4-8, 8-12, etc.) as this number is carefully chosen after experimenting with data multiple times and choosing the bin width that will show the least difference in number of abortions visually.
  • Low-Contrast Beige Palette Score = -1
    The chosen color palatte (a light beige color) will bring less visual significance to the states with higher rates and the ones with lower rates. Other color palattes highlights the difference better which is not what I wanted for this graph.
  • Use of Rate Data: Score = 2
    This is very earnest compared to the previous graph, as I used rate of abortion for each state (measured by number of abortions performed per 1,000 women), which accounts for total women population difference (and thus better rebuttal against the proposition).
  • Open-Ended Top Bin: Score = -1.5
    Choosing to have an open-ended top bin of "16+" lessen the significance of states with the highest rate (NY: 24.6), and simply puts it with the same bin as other states with rate above 16. It makes readers think that the differences in abortion rate is not as significant, as the highest states are simpy categorized into the same bin as states that have a relatively higher rate of 16 or above.

Final Reflection

Working on both sides of the proposition showed me just how much design shapes the story data tells. Creating the “for” graph came naturally—the data already leaned in that direction, so it was mostly about choosing clear visuals that supported what was already visible. But the “against” side was a challenge. I had to actively soften strong patterns by carefully choosing things like muted colors, soft bins, and wording that framed variation as “consistency.” It felt less like visualizing and more like strategy. What surprised me most was how much the ethical line depends on who’s looking. Some choices—like cutting axes—look obviously misleading to people with data experience, but something like evenly spaced bins (which seem innocent) can actually be more deceptive if readers assume equal meaning in each category. That made me realize ethical design isn’t just about avoiding lies—it’s about helping the audience read things as clearly as possible. I now think good visualizations are ones that build in guardrails: adding explanations, clarifying transformations, or at least not making it harder than it should be to see what’s true. In the end, I think persuasive design becomes misleading when it breaks the balance between how much it changes the visual and how much it changes the meaning. If I darken one state by 10% to draw attention, that’s editing. If I darken it so much that it looks like it had five times the value, that’s distortion. The difference is whether a normal viewer could still figure out what’s going on without special knowledge. This project really made me see that every chart is an argument—and ethical visualization is about making sure the data still gets a fair say.
